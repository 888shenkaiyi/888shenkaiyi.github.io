A material analysis by Shenkaiyi Rong

Gender classification, institutional legibility, and the violence of required categories.

Material Object: Gender Classification Form

1. The Object

This project uses a gender classification form as the central material object. The form looks ordinary: it asks a student, applicant, or institutional subject to select Male, Female, or sometimes N/A. But this ordinary object is not neutral. It teaches that gender must be fixed, selected, and made readable before a person can move through an institution.

The form does not simply record gender identity. It produces a narrow version of gender by forcing identity into a small set of administratively acceptable choices. The instruction “check one” becomes a curricular act: it teaches which genders are recognizable, which are treated as exceptions, and which forms of self-definition are blocked before they can appear.

The object’s violence is not only that it asks for gender. It is that the question is often required.
This is not a form that asks for gender. It is a system that produces it.

2. Critical Analysis: What Does the Form Do?

The gender form works as a pedagogical object. It teaches students how gender should be read, how bodies should be classified, and which identities become difficult to recognize.

The gender classification form does not simply record identity; it produces it. By requiring the user to select Male, Female, or N/A, the form teaches that gender must be stable, singular, and institutionally legible.

This form concretely enacts what Wooley (2015) describes as binary gender literacy: it does not simply reflect gender categories, but teaches users how to recognize and reproduce them through limited, institutionalized options. In this sense, the material object itself becomes the site where theory is made visible.

The required nature of the form exemplifies what Slater et al. (2018) describe as biopolitical regulation: gender is not only recognized but managed, as individuals must classify themselves in order to access institutional systems. The form participates in a broader system that governs bodies by making gender classification a condition of visibility and access.

While N/A appears to offer flexibility, it also reveals a contradiction. Rather than fully recognizing gender diversity, it can function as a category of administrative exclusion—marking identities as “not applicable” rather than legitimate.

The presence of N/A also reveals the limits of institutional recognition and aligns with Nicolazzo’s (2020) argument that invisibility can function as both constraint and strategy, especially when visibility is structured by normative expectations. However, the required nature of the form exposes a tension: refusal is not always possible. The system demands legibility, even when that legibility erases complexity.

3. Three Course Texts

The object connects Wooley, Slater et al., and Nicolazzo through one shared problem: institutions often require gender to become readable before a person can become recognized.

Wooley, 2015

Critical Literacy of Binary Gender

Wooley helps show that gender is learned through literacy practices. The form is a gender literacy lesson: it teaches users to read gender as a limited set of official options.

Male/Female becomes a curriculum of binary recognition. N/A may look like an alternative, but it can still position non-binary or unclassifiable gender as outside the main grammar of recognition.

Slater et al., 2018

Biopolitics of Safe Space

Slater et al. help reveal how care and safety can become techniques of regulation. Institutions may classify gender in the name of safety, data, placement, policy, or protection.

But classification also manages bodies. The form turns gender into an administrative object that can be sorted, monitored, and governed.

Nicolazzo, 2020

Invisibility as Possibility

Nicolazzo complicates the assumption that visibility is always liberation. When a form cannot recognize someone’s lived gender, being unseen may become a strategy of protection.

Yet required fields also expose a contradiction: invisibility may be necessary, but the system may not allow refusal. This is where the form becomes coercive.

4. The Interruption

The interruption is not simply adding another category. A third option may appear more inclusive, but it risks reproducing the same logic of classification. A deeper interruption questions why gender must be required at all.

Choose one response.
System waiting for classification...

By checking all boxes, crossing them out, or attempting to leave the field blank, the user exposes the form’s underlying assumption: that gender must be fixed, knowable, and administratively usable.

When the system blocks refusal, it reveals that gender classification is not neutral. It is a condition for recognition and participation.

The interruption is not only exposing the limits of classification, but refusing the assumption that gender must be administratively known at all.

5. Imagining an Alternative Curriculum

A critical curriculum of gender cannot only ask for “better boxes.” It must question why school and institutional systems need gender classification, who benefits from it, and what forms of life become impossible under it.

A critical curriculum of gender would move beyond simply adding more categories. Instead, it would question the necessity of classification itself.

Rather than requiring fixed answers, institutions could allow for self-description, fluidity, or refusal. Gender would no longer function as a gatekeeping mechanism, but as an open, relational, and evolving aspect of identity.

This shift challenges the assumption that institutions must know and categorize gender in order to function. These alternatives also raise a difficult question: whether institutions are willing to give up the need to know, or whether inclusion simply produces new forms of control.

Ask Less

Only collect gender data when there is a clear ethical and pedagogical reason.

Self-Description

Allow students to describe gender in their own language rather than choosing from fixed boxes.

Change Over Time

Treat gender as revisable, not as a permanent administrative truth.

Refusal as Valid

Make “I choose not to answer” a legitimate response, not a blocked pathway.

The poster produces an immediate affective response, while the website translates that experience into theoretical analysis.

6. Author’s Note

I selected the gender classification form because it is ordinary and easy to overlook. It appears as a simple administrative document, yet it carries a powerful lesson about gender. It teaches that gender must be fixed, readable, and available for institutional classification.

My interpretation is shaped by my experience as an international student. In applying for visas, filling out official documents, and undergoing routine registration processes both in China and in the United States, I have repeatedly encountered the same requirement: to select “male” or “female,” and sometimes “N/A.” This repetition reveals that gender classification is not a one-time act, but a continuous institutional demand. The form does not simply ask who I am; it requires me to become legible within its limited categories.

In China, many school, medical, travel, and government records still present gender through a narrow male/female structure. In U.S. institutional contexts, options may appear more flexible, but the structure often remains similar: the user must still make gender answerable to the form. Over time, this repetition produces a feeling that gender must always be available, official, and administratively complete.

My ability to move through these forms without immediate conflict reflects my positionality within systems that privilege binary legibility. While I have experienced the repetition of gender classification across institutional contexts, I recognize that others are more directly constrained, erased, or misrecognized by these systems.

At the same time, I recognize that my experience is not equivalent to those most directly affected by gender classification, including transgender, nonbinary, queer, and gender-nonconforming individuals. My analysis is limited by my own positionality. However, my perspective allows me to see how the normalization of binary gender operates across different institutional contexts, making it appear natural, inevitable, and unquestionable.

The limit of my analysis is that I am focusing on the form as an object, while the lived consequences of gender classification extend far beyond any single document. Still, this object matters because it demonstrates how schools and institutions can turn gender into an administrative fact before individuals have the opportunity to define it for themselves.
